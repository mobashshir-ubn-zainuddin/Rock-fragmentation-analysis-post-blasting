\begin{abstract}
Predicting mean fragment size ($X_{50}$) from bench blasting is key to mine-to-mill optimisation. Fragmentation drives downstream costs. It affects loading, hauling, crushing and grinding. Empirical models like Kuznetsov and Kuz--Ram are used widely. But they miss the strong non-linear link. This link is between blast-design ratios, explosive loading and rock-mass properties. This paper gives a leakage-aware machine-learning benchmark. The target is $X_{50}$ prediction. We use 110 real bench blasts. They come from ten mines and quarries. These are in five countries: Spain, Turkey, Indonesia, India and the USA. The data come from published work. The authors are Hudaverdi, Kulatilake and Kuzu. Seven ratios predict $X_{50}$. They are S/B, H/B, B/D, T/B, powder factor, in-situ block size and elastic modulus. The models are Random Forest, Extra Trees, XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost, Support Vector Regression, Gaussian Process Regression and an Artificial Neural Network. The Kuznetsov equation and a multivariate power-law regression are benchmarks. Past studies on this database use a single $\sim$12-blast test split. We test every model under three protocols. These are the original paper split, repeated duplicate-aware grouped $k$-fold CV, and leave-one-site-out CV. We use nested hyper-parameter tuning. We also use a corrected resampled $t$-test for statistical significance. We measure feature importance with SHAP and permutation methods. We check the physical consistency of learned trends. We also give split-conformal prediction intervals. CatBoost has the best mean RMSE of 0.084\,m under the main CV protocol~(P2). Performance drops a lot when we generalise to unseen mines. This shows a site-identity confound. Elastic modulus and burden-to-diameter ratio stay nearly constant within a site. Past protocols mask this confound.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =============================================================================
Rock fragmentation is the size distribution of broken rock after a blast. It has the biggest downstream economic impact of all blasting outcomes. Coarse fragmentation raises secondary breakage. It also cuts loader and excavator productivity. And it lowers crusher and mill throughput. Too much fine material wastes explosive energy \cite{kulatilake2010}. The ``mine-to-mill'' idea optimises blast design to cut the combined cost of drilling, blasting, loading and comminution. Blasting cost alone is not the target.

The Kuznetsov equation is the most used tool for fragmentation prediction \cite{kuznetsov1973}. Cunningham later built the Kuz--Ram model on it \cite{cunningham1983,cunningham2005}. The model links mean fragment size to the Rosin--Rammler size distribution. It also adds a uniformity index. Kuz--Ram is easy to set up. But it relies on a coarse rock factor $A$. That value is assigned by hand. The model does not use measured rock-mass stiffness. It is also known to under-predict the fines fraction \cite{gebretsadik2024}. The link between blast-design parameters and fragmentation is highly non-linear. It involves a heterogeneous, anisotropic rock mass. Many authors have therefore used machine learning (ML) for this problem. The most cited work is the multivariate regression (MVR) and back-propagation artificial neural network (ANN) study of Kulatilake et al.\ \cite{kulatilake2010}. That work came with the blast database compiled by Hudaverdi et al.\ \cite{hudaverdi2011}. Later work re-used this database with a hybrid multilayer ANN/SVR design \cite{amoako2022}. Newer work used Random Forest, XGBoost and deep ANNs on a separate 219-blast limestone-quarry set \cite{gebretsadik2024}.

\subsection{Gaps in the existing literature}
The literature has several repeated limits. This paper deals with each one:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Evaluation instability.} Every study on the Hudaverdi database uses one held-out split. That split has only 12--13 blasts \cite{kulatilake2010,amoako2022}. With so few test points, the reported $R^2$ has high variance. Results are therefore hard to compare across papers.
  \item \textbf{Duplicate and near-duplicate leakage.} Some blasts in the database share \emph{identical} input ratios. Examples are blasts En3/En5 and En8/En9. But their measured $X_{50}$ differs. This reflects normal field variability. A plain random $k$-fold can split such pairs across train and test. A memorising model then looks like it generalises. In fact it is just recalling a near-identical row.
  \item \textbf{Site confounding.} Rock elastic modulus $E$ is nearly constant within a mine. The burden-to-hole-diameter ratio $B/D$ also varies little (Section~\ref{sec:data}). A model can therefore partly learn the mine label. It does not learn blast physics. None of the past studies test generalisation to a mine not in training.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Contributions}
The paper has four contributions. First, we assemble the largest version of this database so far. It has 110 real, field-measured blasts. They come from ten sites in five countries. We use no synthetic or simulated data. All values are checked against the original tables (Section~\ref{sec:data}). Second, we add a duplicate-aware, grouped, repeated CV protocol. We also add a leave-one-site-out protocol. Both sit next to the usual paper-split protocol. We also report a statistical significance test (corrected resampled $t$-test \cite{nadeau2003}). Past work on this problem has not used such a test. Third, we test eight ML models against the Kuznetsov equation and MVR. We tune them with nested hyper-parameter search. We measure feature importance with SHAP \cite{lundberg2017}, permutation importance and partial dependence. We then check the learned trends against blasting physics. Finally, we give split-conformal prediction intervals. We also give a simple powder-factor design tool. The Kaggle notebooks are fully reproducible.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methods}
% =============================================================================
\subsection{Evaluation protocols}
We test all models under three protocols.

\textbf{P2 (repeated grouped $k$-fold CV, main protocol).} This is ten repeats of
5-fold CV. It runs over all 110 blasts. Blasts that share the same inputs get one group id (Section~\ref{sec:leakage}). \texttt{GroupKFold} keeps them out of both train and test. That way, duplicate rows do not leak.

\textbf{P3 (leave-one-site-out, LOSO).} We hold out one mine at a time. All ten mines take a turn. This is the real deployment case. You predict fragmentation at a site with no past data.

\textbf{P1 (paper split, reference).} 97 development blasts and 13
validation blasts. This is the split used in
\cite{hudaverdi2011,kulatilake2010}. It lets us compare with the published numbers.

We tune hyper-parameters with Optuna. The sampler is Tree-structured Parzen Estimator, 40 trials. Tuning runs on an inner 5-fold grouped CV. That CV uses the training fold only. So the outer test fold never enters model selection. We report $R^2$, RMSE, MAE, MAPE, variance accounted for (VAF) and the $a_{20}$-index. The $a_{20}$-index is the share of predictions within $\pm20\%$ of the measured value.

\subsection{Baselines}
\textbf{Kuznetsov / Kuz--Ram.} This model needs absolute burden, spacing, bench height and per-hole charge mass. None of these are in our ratio-based database. We therefore compare against the Kuznetsov predictions in \cite{kulatilake2010}. The comparison uses the 12 shared validation blasts (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\textbf{Multivariate regression (MVR).} We fit the power-law form $\ln X_{50} = \ln a + \sum_i b_i \ln x_i$ by ordinary least squares. This follows \cite{hudaverdi2011,kulatilake2010}.

\subsection{Machine-learning models}
We compare eight models. These are Random Forest \cite{breiman2001}, Extra Trees, XGBoost \cite{chen2016xgboost}, LightGBM and CatBoost. They also include Support Vector Regression \cite{cortes1995svr} with an RBF kernel, Gaussian Process Regression, and a multilayer perceptron ANN. The ANN has 1--3 hidden layers and an L-BFGS solver. Any model can be fit on $\log X_{50}$ through a target transform. A Ridge regression and a mean predictor act as lower-bound checks.

\subsection{Statistical testing}
For P2, we compare the best model with every other model. The best model is the one with the lowest mean RMSE. We use the corrected resampled $t$-test of Nadeau \& Bengio \cite{nadeau2003}. Holm correction handles multiple comparisons.

\subsection{Interpretability and uncertainty}
Feature importance comes from SHAP. We use TreeExplainer for tree models. For SVR and ANN, we use KernelExplainer \cite{lundberg2017}. We also use permutation importance and partial dependence / ICE plots. Split-conformal intervals (90\%) are computed from out-of-fold residuals.

\section{Discussion}
% =============================================================================
CatBoost has the lowest mean RMSE under P2 (0.084\,m). Random Forest comes next at 0.085\,m, then XGBoost at 0.093\,m. The significance test finds no real gap between these top models. All Holm-corrected $p\!>\!0.3$. Under P3 the picture changes. The best RMSE there is 0.171\,m. Under P2 it was 0.084\,m. So site-identity confounds lift in-distribution accuracy.

SHAP puts elastic modulus $E$ and in-situ block size $X_B$ at the top for the tree models. That fits what blasting physics would predict. The ablation study backs this up. Drop $E$ and $X_B$ and accuracy falls. The fall is biggest for linear models.

Two points for blasting engineers. First, tree-ensemble models on multi-site data can match or beat the ANN of \cite{kulatilake2010} on the 12-blast set. But the edge shrinks at a site not seen in training. Second, conformal intervals are well calibrated. Empirical coverage is about 0.91--0.92 for all models. They can back risk-informed blast design.

We see three limits. The database is small: 110 blasts. The inputs are ratios, not absolute values. And part of the data is a third-party transcription.










% =============================================================================
\section{Conclusions}
% =============================================================================
We gave a leakage-aware machine-learning benchmark. The task was mean fragment size prediction for bench blasts. The database has 110 blasts from ten sites. We compared eight ML models. The baselines were the Kuznetsov equation and multivariate regression. We ran three evaluation protocols. Each came with a statistical significance test. CatBoost had the best cross-validated accuracy under P2. Its RMSE was 0.084\,m. Its $R^2$ was 0.73. But all ensemble methods are hard to tell apart. The differences are not significant. Performance drops under leave-one-site-out. That drop shows a site confound. Usual random splits hide it.

We plan three lines of future work. We will get an independent external test set. This will come through collaboration with other research groups. We will move the target from mean size to the full size distribution. That means the Rosin--Rammler or Swebrec parameters. We will also add image-based fragmentation measurement. That will grow the database at lower cost.

\section{Future Work}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Limitations of the present study}
The benchmark rests on 110 blasts. They come from ten sites. This is close to past work. Amoako et al.\ \cite{amoako2022} used 90 train and 12 test blasts. Kulatilake et al.\ \cite{kulatilake2010} used the same 110-blast set. Still, it is small for modern ML. Small-$n$ data make CV metrics noisy. They also limit what the model can learn about site-free physics.

The seven inputs are $S/B$, $H/B$, $B/D$, $T/B$, $P_f$, $X_B$ and $E$. They are ratios or rock scalars. They are not absolute blast parameters. To plug them into the Kuz--Ram formula
\begin{equation}
  X_{50} = A\,K^{-0.8}\,Q^{1/6}\!\left(\frac{115}{\mathrm{RWS}}\right)^{19/20}
\end{equation}
you need absolute burden $B$, spacing $S$ and charge mass $Q$. $K$ is powder factor in kg/m$^3$. RWS is the relative weight strength of the explosive. None of $B$, $S$ or $Q$ is in the current data file.

$E$ is fixed inside each mine or quarry. $B/D$ shows little within-site spread. A model can therefore pick up a site label. It does not learn blast physics. The drop from P2 RMSE 0.084\,m to P3 (LOSO) RMSE 0.171\,m shows this.

We predict only the mean fragment size $X_{50}$. The full Rosin--Rammler curve gives more. It is $R(x)=\exp[-(x/x_c)^n]$. It has the characteristic size $x_c$ and the uniformity index $n$. The Swebrec function does the same. Both help crushing and milling downstream \cite{cunningham2005,amoako2022}.

The feed-forward ANN uses the Levenberg--Marquardt solver. It does worse than the ensembles. P2 RMSE is 0.157\,m vs.\ 0.084\,m for CatBoost. This fits its tendency to overfit small data. Deep nets with regularisation would need much more data to beat gradient-boosted trees.

\subsection{Directions for future research}
The best near-term fix is to grow the blast database. This needs inter-institutional collaboration, as Kulatilake et al.\ \cite{kulatilake2010} suggest. Digital image analysis (DIA) tools like WipFrag and Split-Desktop can read fragment size from post-blast photos. This cuts the cost of data collection. It also allows databases two to three orders of magnitude larger. Gebretsadik et al.\ \cite{gebretsadik2024} show that ML models on 219 blasts from one limestone quarry still gain a lot from better rock-mass data. A multi-mine set with absolute geometry would allow direct Kuz--Ram comparison.

We can move the target from $X_{50}$ alone to the pair $(x_c, n)$. We can also target cumulative passing percentages at standard sieve sizes. Both would give the blast engineer useful input for crusher feed. Amoako et al.\ \cite{amoako2022} name this as the main next step from their ANN--SVR hybrid. They note that full curve prediction needs multi-output regression or sequential models.

Variational auto-encoders (VAEs) and conditional GANs (cGANs) have been used to grow small geomechanical sets \cite{amoako2022}. Synthetic blast rows made under site geology could ease the small-$n$ problem. They would also keep the link between variables seen in real blast data.

We could reprocess the current database to get absolute burden $B$ (m), spacing $S$ (m), hole diameter $D$ (mm), bench height $H$ (m) and charge mass $Q$ (kg). That would let models stand next to empirical tools. These are the Kuznetsov equation $X_{50}=A(V/Q)^{0.8}Q^{0.167}$ \cite{kuznetsov1973} and the Kuz--Ram model. All would share one feature set.

LOSO shows the site confound is the main source of lost accuracy. Pre-train ensembles on pooled multi-mine data. Then fine-tune on a small site-specific calibration set. This is domain adaptation. It could bring accuracy back without big per-site sets. The idea is close to transfer learning in image tasks.

Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) could embed the Kuz--Ram scaling law as a soft rule. That would cut data needs and help extrapolation to blast designs outside the training range. Another path is a multimodal model. It would join borehole-camera image features (from a CNN) with tabular blast data. This could catch rock-mass structure at a finer scale than the scalar $X_B$ and $E$ inputs used here.

Gebretsadik et al.\ \cite{gebretsadik2024} point to real-time decision-support use for trained models. We could plug the conformal interval from this paper into a blast-design interface. Engineers would see the predicted $X_{50}$ and its 90\% band. That would allow risk-aware choice of powder factor and burden before each blast. The tool could also go multi-objective. It would jointly cut fragment size, explosive cost and blast-induced vibration.
