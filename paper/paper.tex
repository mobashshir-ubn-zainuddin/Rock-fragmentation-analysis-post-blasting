\begin{abstract}
Accurate prediction of the mean fragment size ($X_{50}$) from bench blasting is central to mine-to-mill optimisation, because fragmentation governs downstream loading, hauling, crushing and grinding costs. Empirical models such as the Kuznetsov and Kuz--Ram equations are widely used, but they do not capture the strongly non-linear relationship between blast-design ratios, explosive loading and rock-mass properties. This paper presents a leakage-aware machine-learning benchmark for $X_{50}$ prediction using a database of 110 real bench blasts from ten mines and quarries in five countries (Spain, Turkey, Indonesia, India and the USA), compiled from the published work of Hudaverdi, Kulatilake and Kuzu. Seven blast-design, explosive and rock-property ratios (S/B, H/B, B/D, T/B, powder factor, in-situ block size and elastic modulus) are used to predict $X_{50}$ with Random Forest, Extra Trees, XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost, Support Vector Regression, Gaussian Process Regression and an Artificial Neural Network, benchmarked against the Kuznetsov equation and a multivariate power-law regression. Prior studies on this database report a single $\sim$12-blast test split. We evaluate every model under three protocols: the original paper split, repeated duplicate-aware grouped $k$-fold cross-validation, and leave-one-site-out cross-validation, with nested hyper-parameter tuning and a corrected resampled $t$-test for statistical significance. Feature importance is quantified with SHAP and permutation methods, the physical consistency of the learned trends is checked, and split-conformal prediction intervals are provided. CatBoost achieves the best mean RMSE of 0.084\,m under the main cross-validation protocol~(P2), and performance degrades substantially when generalising to unseen mines, which exposes a site-identity confound (elastic modulus and burden-to-diameter ratio are nearly constant within a site) that is masked by protocols used in prior work.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =============================================================================
Rock fragmentation is the size distribution of broken rock produced by a
blast, and it is the single blasting outcome with the largest downstream
economic impact: coarse fragmentation increases secondary breakage, reduces
loader and excavator productivity, and lowers crusher and mill throughput,
while excessively fine fragmentation wastes explosive energy
\cite{kulatilake2010}. The ``mine-to-mill'' philosophy explicitly optimises
blast design to minimise the combined cost of drilling, blasting, loading
and comminution instead of blasting cost alone.

The most widely used engineering tool for fragmentation prediction is the
Kuznetsov equation \cite{kuznetsov1973}, later developed into the Kuz--Ram
model by Cunningham \cite{cunningham1983,cunningham2005}, which combines an
empirical mean-fragment-size relation with the Rosin--Rammler size
distribution and a uniformity index. Although easy to parameterise, Kuz--Ram
relies on a coarse, subjectively assigned rock factor $A$, does not use
measured rock-mass stiffness, and is known to under-predict the fines
fraction \cite{gebretsadik2024}. The true relationship between
blast-design parameters and fragmentation is highly non-linear and involves
a heterogeneous, anisotropic rock mass. Several authors have therefore
applied machine learning (ML) to this problem, most influentially the
multivariate regression (MVR) and back-propagation artificial neural network
(ANN) study of Kulatilake et al.\ \cite{kulatilake2010} and the underlying
blast database compiled by Hudaverdi et al.\ \cite{hudaverdi2011}.
Subsequent studies re-used this database with a hybrid multilayer ANN/SVR
architecture \cite{amoako2022}, and newer studies applied Random Forest,
XGBoost and deep ANNs to a separate 219-blast limestone-quarry dataset
\cite{gebretsadik2024}.

\subsection{Gaps in the existing literature}
The existing literature has recurring limitations that this paper addresses:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Evaluation instability.} Every study on the Hudaverdi
  database reports results on a single held-out split of only 12--13
  blasts \cite{kulatilake2010,amoako2022}. With so few test points,
  the reported $R^2$ has very high variance and is not directly comparable
  across papers.
  \item \textbf{Duplicate and near-duplicate leakage.} Several blasts in
  the database share \emph{identical} input ratios (e.g.\ blasts En3/En5,
  En8/En9) but different measured $X_{50}$, reflecting natural field
  variability. If an ordinary random $k$-fold splits such duplicates across
  train and test folds, a memorising model can appear to generalise while
  recalling a near-identical training row.
  \item \textbf{Site confounding.} Rock elastic modulus $E$ and the
  burden-to-hole-diameter ratio $B/D$ are almost constant within a given
  mine (Section~\ref{sec:data}), so a model can partly recognise the mine
  instead of learning blast physics. None of the prior studies test
  generalisation to a mine absent from training.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Contributions}
This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item We assemble the largest available version of this database (110 real,
  field-measured blasts, ten sites, five countries; no synthetic or simulated
  data), cross-checked against originally published tables
  (Section~\ref{sec:data}).
  \item We introduce a duplicate-aware, grouped, repeated
  cross-validation protocol and a leave-one-site-out protocol alongside the
  conventional paper-split protocol, and report a statistical significance
  test (corrected resampled $t$-test \cite{nadeau2003}) between models;
  this test is absent from all prior work on this problem.
  \item We benchmark eight ML regressors against the Kuznetsov equation and
  MVR with nested hyper-parameter tuning, and quantify feature importance
  with SHAP \cite{lundberg2017}, permutation importance and partial
  dependence, checking learned trends against blasting-physics expectations.
  \item We provide split-conformal prediction intervals and a simple
  powder-factor design tool, with fully reproducible Kaggle notebooks.
\end{itemize}

Several blasts share identical values of all seven inputs but differ in

field variability. Site clustering also affects the data, since $E$ is
constant within each mine by construction and $B/D$ varies little within a

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methods}
% =============================================================================
\subsection{Evaluation protocols}
All models are evaluated under three protocols.

\textbf{P2 (repeated grouped $k$-fold CV, main protocol).} Ten repeats of
5-fold cross-validation over all 110 blasts. Blasts sharing identical
inputs (Section~\ref{sec:leakage}) are assigned to a common group id and
\texttt{GroupKFold} guarantees they are never split across train/test,
eliminating duplicate-row leakage.

\textbf{P3 (leave-one-site-out, LOSO).} Each of the ten mines is held out
in turn; this is the realistic deployment scenario of predicting
fragmentation at a site with no training history.

\textbf{P1 (paper split, reference).} 97 development blasts / 13
original validation blasts, reproducing the split used in
\cite{hudaverdi2011,kulatilake2010} for direct comparison with published
numbers.

Hyper-parameters are tuned with Optuna (Tree-structured Parzen Estimator,
40 trials) on an inner 5-fold grouped cross-validation of the
training fold only, so the outer test fold is never used for model
selection. Reported metrics are $R^2$, RMSE, MAE, MAPE, variance accounted
for (VAF) and the $a_{20}$-index (fraction of predictions within $\pm20\%$
of the measured value).

\subsection{Baselines}
\textbf{Kuznetsov / Kuz--Ram.} This model requires absolute burden,
spacing, bench height and per-hole charge mass, which are not present in
the ratio-based database; we therefore compare against the Kuznetsov
predictions published in \cite{kulatilake2010} for the 12 shared
validation blasts (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\textbf{Multivariate regression (MVR).} Following
\cite{hudaverdi2011,kulatilake2010}, we fit the power-law form
$\ln X_{50} = \ln a + \sum_i b_i \ln x_i$ by ordinary least squares.

\subsection{Machine-learning models}
Random Forest \cite{breiman2001}, Extra Trees, XGBoost
\cite{chen2016xgboost}, LightGBM, CatBoost, Support Vector Regression
\cite{cortes1995svr} (RBF kernel), Gaussian Process Regression, and a
multilayer perceptron ANN (1--3 hidden layers, L-BFGS solver) are compared,
each optionally fit on $\log X_{50}$ via a target transform. A Ridge
regression and a mean predictor serve as lower-bound sanity checks.

\subsection{Statistical testing}
For the P2 protocol, we compare the best model (lowest mean RMSE) against
every other model using the corrected resampled $t$-test of Nadeau \&
Bengio \cite{nadeau2003}, with Holm correction for multiple comparisons.

\subsection{Interpretability and uncertainty}
Feature importance is quantified with SHAP TreeExplainer (tree models) and
KernelExplainer (SVR, ANN) \cite{lundberg2017}, permutation importance, and
partial dependence / ICE plots. Split-conformal prediction intervals (90\%)
are computed from out-of-fold residuals.

\section{Discussion}
% =============================================================================
CatBoost achieves the lowest mean RMSE (0.084\,m) under P2, followed
closely by Random Forest (0.085\,m) and XGBoost (0.093\,m); the
significance test shows no statistically significant difference between the
top ensemble methods (all Holm-corrected $p\!>\!0.3$). Performance degrades
substantially under P3 (best RMSE 0.171\,m vs.\ 0.084\,m under P2), which
shows that site-identity confounds inflate in-distribution accuracy.
SHAP analysis ranks elastic modulus $E$ and in-situ block size $X_B$ as
the most influential features across tree-based models, in line with
blasting-physics expectations. The ablation study shows that removing
rock-property features ($E$, $X_B$) degrades accuracy substantially,
particularly for linear models. Two recommendations follow for blasting
engineers. Tree-ensemble models trained on multi-site databases can match
or exceed the ANN of \cite{kulatilake2010} at the 12-blast validation set,
but their advantage is reduced when tested at an unseen site. Conformal
intervals are well-calibrated (empirical coverage $\approx 0.91$--$0.92$
for all models) and can support risk-informed blast design. Limitations
include the small database size (110 blasts), reliance on ratio-based
rather than absolute inputs, and the third-party transcription of part of
the database.












% =============================================================================
\section{Conclusions}
% =============================================================================
This paper presented a leakage-aware machine-learning benchmark for
bench-blast mean fragment size prediction on a 110-blast, ten-site,
real-world database, comparing eight ML models against the Kuznetsov
equation and multivariate regression under three complementary evaluation
protocols with statistical significance testing. CatBoost achieved the
best cross-validated accuracy (RMSE 0.084\,m, $R^2$ 0.73 under P2), but
all ensemble methods are statistically indistinguishable from one another.
Performance degrades markedly under leave-one-site-out evaluation, which
exposes a site confound masked by conventional random splits. Future work
includes obtaining an independent external test set through collaboration
with other research groups, extending the target from mean fragment size to
the full size distribution (Rosin--Rammler / Swebrec parameters), and
incorporating image-based fragmentation measurement to enlarge the
database at lower cost.

\section{Future Work}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Limitations of the present study}
The benchmark rests on 110 blasts drawn from ten sites. This is comparable to previous studies, where Amoako et al.\ \cite{amoako2022} used 90 training and 12 test blasts and Kulatilake et al.\ \cite{kulatilake2010} employed the same 110-blast corpus, but it remains small by modern ML standards. Small-$n$ settings amplify variance in cross-validated metrics and limit the capacity to learn site-agnostic physics. The seven inputs ($S/B$, $H/B$, $B/D$, $T/B$, $P_f$, $X_B$, $E$) are dimensionless ratios or rock-property scalars instead of absolute blast parameters. Direct substitution into the Kuz--Ram formula
\begin{equation}
  X_{50} = A\,K^{-0.8}\,Q^{1/6}\!\left(\frac{115}{\mathrm{RWS}}\right)^{19/20}
\end{equation}
(where $K$ is the powder factor in kg/m$^3$ and RWS is the relative weight strength of the explosive) requires absolute burden $B$, spacing $S$, and charge mass $Q$, which are not available in the current transcription of the dataset. Elastic modulus $E$ is constant within each mine/quarry and $B/D$ shows limited within-site variation. Consequently, models may partly learn a site identifier instead of generalising blast-physics relationships; the pronounced drop from P2 RMSE 0.084\,m to P3 (LOSO) RMSE 0.171\,m shows this. Only the mean fragment size $X_{50}$ is predicted. The full Rosin--Rammler fragment-size distribution $R(x)=\exp[-(x/x_c)^n]$ (characterised by both the characteristic size $x_c$ and uniformity index $n$) or the Swebrec function provides richer information for downstream crushing and milling optimisation \cite{cunningham2005,amoako2022}. The feed-forward ANN trained with the Levenberg--Marquardt algorithm underperforms ensemble methods (P2 RMSE 0.157\,m vs.\ 0.084\,m for CatBoost), which is consistent with its susceptibility to overfitting on small databases. Deep architectures with regularisation would require substantially larger corpora to outperform gradient-boosted trees.

\subsection{Directions for future research}
The most impactful near-term improvement is enlarging the blast database through inter-institutional collaboration, following the recommendation of Kulatilake et al.\ \cite{kulatilake2010}. Digital image analysis (DIA) methods such as WipFrag and Split-Desktop can automate fragment measurement from post-blast photographs, reducing the cost of data acquisition and enabling databases two to three orders of magnitude larger. Gebretsadik et al.\ \cite{gebretsadik2024} demonstrate that ML models trained on 219 blasts from a single limestone quarry still benefit significantly from richer rock-mass characterisation; a multi-mine database with absolute geometric inputs would enable direct Kuz--Ram comparison.

Extending the regression target from $X_{50}$ alone to the pair $(x_c, n)$ of the Rosin--Rammler distribution, or directly to cumulative passing percentages at standardised sieve sizes, would provide the blast-design engineer with actionable information for crusher feed optimisation. Amoako et al.\ \cite{amoako2022} identify this as the primary future direction from their ANN--SVR hybrid work, noting that full distribution prediction requires either multi-output regression or sequential models.

Variational auto-encoders (VAEs) and conditional generative adversarial networks (cGANs) have been proposed as a route to augmenting small geomechanical datasets \cite{amoako2022}. Generating synthetic blast records conditioned on site geology could alleviate the small-$n$ limitation while preserving the inter-variable correlations present in physical blast data.

Reprocessing or supplementing the existing database to recover absolute values of burden $B$ (m), spacing $S$ (m), hole diameter $D$ (mm), bench height $H$ (m), and charge mass $Q$ (kg) would allow models to be evaluated alongside empirical predictors such as the Kuznetsov equation $X_{50}=A(V/Q)^{0.8}Q^{0.167}$ \cite{kuznetsov1973} and the Kuz--Ram model on a fully unified feature set.

Leave-one-site-out evaluation reveals that the site confound is the dominant source of generalisation loss. Pre-training ensemble models on pooled multi-mine data and fine-tuning on a small site-specific calibration set (domain adaptation) could recover accuracy without requiring large per-site datasets, an approach analogous to transfer learning in image classification.

Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) that embed the Kuz--Ram scaling law as a soft constraint could reduce data requirements and improve extrapolation to blast designs outside the training distribution. Alternatively, coupling borehole-camera image features (extracted by a CNN) with tabular blast parameters in a multimodal model could capture rock-mass heterogeneity at a finer spatial scale than the scalar $X_B$ and $E$ inputs used here.

Gebretsadik et al.\ \cite{gebretsadik2024} highlight the potential for deploying trained models as real-time decision-support tools. Integrating the conformal prediction interval presented here into a blast-design interface, so that engineers see the predicted $X_{50}$ together with its 90\% uncertainty band, would allow risk-informed specification of powder factor and burden before each blast. Such a tool could be extended to multi-objective optimisation, jointly minimising fragment size, explosive cost, and blast-induced vibration.
